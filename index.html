Under the hood

A grounded engine today. Two purpose-built models next.

ResumeFit makes no calls to an LLM API. Version 1 is a deterministic engine with a verifier, so it can't invent experience you don't have. Each stage below gets replaced by a model of its own only after that model clears a ship gate I set before training it.

Decisions and what they cost

Two small models, not one big prompt

Because
Scoring is classification. Suggesting is constrained writing. They have different metrics and costs, the score feeds the writer, and each can fail and roll back on its own.
Tradeoff
More to build. At low volume, one API call (about half a cent a check) would ship sooner.

Qwen3-0.6B over the newer Qwen3.5-0.8B

Because
On my benchmark, Qwen3.5's linear-attention layers had no fast kernels on Apple GPUs and trained at under 3 tokens/s.
Tradeoff
Slightly lower published benchmark scores, for a model that trains and serves on any stack.

Synthetic data over the best public dataset

Because
The strongest public set, 8,000 labelled pairs, declares no license. A model trained on it can't be published.
Tradeoff
About $9 for license-clean data and labels (estimate), checked against 50 pairs I label by hand.

A verifier in code, not a promise in a prompt

Because
A prompt can lower the hallucination rate. Only code can guarantee that every edit quotes your resume.
Tradeoff
Some good edits get dropped. For advice about your career, that's the right side to err on.

Ship gates

  1. v1Live

    Rules engine and grounding verifier.

    The baseline every model has to beat.

  2. v2Scorer

    Replaces v1 scoring only if macro-F1 on my 50-pair gold set beats the best baseline, sentence embeddings included, by at least 0.05, with Spearman ≥ 0.6.

    If it doesn't, the embedding baseline ships and the write-up says so.

  3. v3Writer

    Replaces v1 suggestions only if the hallucination rate is ≤ 2% after the verifier and it wins ≥ 60% of pairwise judgments against the prompted base model.

    Judge calibrated against my own ratings, with answer order randomised.

How scoring works

ResumeFit finds the skills and tools a job asks for, separates core requirements from nice-to-haves, and checks which ones your resume shows. Core requirements count twice as much as nice-to-haves. The score is 100 × P(strong) + 50 × P(partial), so it moves smoothly between tiers instead of jumping.

Every suggestion quotes the line in your resume it's based on. If ResumeFit can't point to a line, it doesn't make the suggestion. Skills missing from your resume are listed as gaps, never as things to add.

Today this runs as version 1, a rules engine in your browser. The trained models and the gates they must pass are described under How it's built.

ResumeFit is a tool for job seekers working on their own resume. It isn't designed or tested for screening candidates.
